4.1 Safety Considerations Overview

This chapter covers five topics:

  • Static-field projectile and torque hazards, including screening and access control
  • Gradient-field peripheral nerve stimulation and acoustic noise
  • RF heating and burn prevention
  • Magnet quenches and cryogen safety
  • Real-world accidents

4.2 Main Magnetic Field (\(B_0\)): General Properties

Human body-size MR scanners are most commonly operated at 1.5 T and 3 T, with a smaller number at 7 T and very few at 9.4 T or above. Field homogeneity and stability are scanner-specific specifications, normally reported in ppm over a stated diameter spherical volume. MR physicists also use small-bore systems at much higher field strengths for animal imaging and chemical spectroscopy; the smaller imaging volume makes high field and stringent homogeneity more tractable.

4.3 Ferromagnetic Objects and Projectile Hazards

The persistent static field creates two related hazards: the projectile effect, in which a ferromagnetic object is pulled toward the bore, and torque, which tends to rotate an elongated ferromagnetic object into alignment with the field. Common hazardous objects include oxygen canisters, IV poles, tools, and scissors, as well as many iron- or steel-containing items. The magnet remains at field between scans and overnight, so screening and control of people and objects entering the MR environment are essential.

When operating conditions and screening procedures are followed, temporary exposure to the MR environment has no known health hazard. The static field can nevertheless move or torque unsafe objects and implants. Subjects moving quickly through a strong field may report transient dizziness, a metallic taste, or visual flickers. Rapidly switching gradient fields can induce peripheral nerve stimulation and produce acoustic noise that requires hearing protection. RF energy can heat tissue and conductive materials; burn prevention requires attention to patient positioning, cables, conductive loops, implants, and the sequence-specific SAR limits (Section 3.1).

4.4 Screening, Labels, and Patient Protection

Every patient, visitor, and item entering the MR environment must be screened according to site policy. Implants and devices must be positively identified and their MR Safe, MR Conditional, or MR Unsafe status verified. An MR Conditional device may be used only under its specified field strength, spatial-gradient, RF, and operating conditions. Padding, cable management, avoidance of skin-to-skin conductive loops, and appropriate hearing protection reduce avoidable RF-burn and acoustic-injury risks.

4.6 Magnet Quench

A superconducting magnet operating in persistent mode can maintain current without continuous electrical power to its windings, but its cryogenic support system still requires power. A quench is a sudden loss of superconductivity: resistance develops, stored magnetic energy is converted to heat, and the resulting heating can drive a rapid transition through the winding. Cooling failure is one possible trigger; mechanical, electrical, or other disturbances can also initiate a quench. In helium-bath systems, the deposited heat vaporizes liquid helium rapidly and can produce a large volume of cold gas.

Helium is inert, but a quench can create cold-injury, pressure, oxygen-displacement, and visibility hazards. The exhaust and emergency response arrangements depend on the magnet and installation; personnel must follow the site’s MR-safety and quench procedures rather than assume that a release is harmless.

4.8 The Size of Magnetic Forces

The forces involved near a high-field magnet are difficult to appreciate without direct experience. The displacement force on a ferromagnetic object depends on the static field and its spatial gradient; torque also depends on the object’s shape and orientation. These effects can change rapidly in the fringe field, so an object that seems manageable at one location can become hazardous nearer the bore. Even experienced MRI staff must maintain strict protocols to prevent accidents.

Many open-platform scanners use a vertical field geometry with a gap between the upper and lower magnet assemblies. Their lower field strength often reduces the available SNR under otherwise comparable conditions, but the open geometry can improve surgical access and patient tolerance. The appropriate scanner depends on the clinical task and the required image quality.

Even relatively low-field systems can present substantial projectile and torque hazards. Safety precautions depend on the scanner’s field map and the object’s material, shape, and location—not on nominal field strength alone.
