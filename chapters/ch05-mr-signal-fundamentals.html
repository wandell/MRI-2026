5.1 Fundamental MR measurements

In this section we introduce a fundamental magnetic-resonance experiment, the free induction decay (FID). To understand this experiment, we only need to consider two parts of the instrument: the main magnetic field and the radiofrequency coils. We will explain the use of imaging gradients in later chapters.

But I want you to see where we are headed. So before I describe the FID, let’s have a look at the console, where a user sits down to set up the parameters for an MRI experiment. We are going to build up our understanding from the very simple FID experiment to a system that can make an extraordinary range of measurements.

At the console, the user defines a program that controls the RF signal and the imaging gradients. The orchestration of these signals—their amplitudes and timing—is called pulse-sequence programming. The three tabs show screenshots with the various parameters we set to control the pulse sequence.

5.3 MR pulse sequences

This diagram shows a conventional way to represent a pulse sequence. It represents the controls for a classic sequence called spin echo, which we will study later. For now, notice that the horizontal axis represents time, increasing from left to right. Each line shows how we control one sequence component, such as a gradient or the RF excitation.

By choosing different parameter timings, we can acquire signals with different sensitivities to tissue properties. The pulse sequence is adjusted to highlight certain differences between tissues and diminish others.

The MR images that we produce are not at all what you see if you look at the brain with your eye. MR images are reconstructed from electrical signals detected after RF excitation. Those signals depend on tissue properties, allowing MRI to make otherwise invisible tissue differences visible.

5.5 MR pulse sequence parameter choices

Altering the values of the parameters produces signals with different sensitivities to physical properties of the sample.

In this image array, the authors used a spin-echo sequence while varying the timing parameters (TE and TR). This is often a useful way to understand a system.

5.6 To the future – and beyond

Much is known about the relationship between the sequence, its parameters, and the underlying tissue. But this field is certainly not fully worked out. New discoveries continue to be made and people invent new ideas about pulse sequences that enable us to measure new things. One of the main topics of this course, measuring functional signals from the brain, was only invented thirty years ago. Many new ideas continue to emerge.

Those ideas will be built upon the foundations that we do understand today. The most fundamental observation is the free induction decay (FID) experiment. We introduce this experiment in the next section.

5.7 Free induction decay

  • The substrate
  • The equipment
  • The signal

We are now ready for the free induction decay measurement, the simplest MR signal experiment. The idea of the experiment is built up in the series of images in this panel.

We place a beaker of water, in this example, inside a uniform magnetic field. We then apply an RF pulse at a chosen frequency. After the pulse, the coil detects a voltage induced by the sample. That’s it.

Remember—at this point in our understanding, we are not measuring an image. We are measuring one time-varying voltage signal from a beaker of water. It will be a little while before we get to the topic of how we might form an image. The following material is all about measurements from a single volume.
