This chapter is about how the shape of the brain — its folded, topologically complex surface — shapes the choices we make when we smooth, average, and visualize MRI data. The theme running through the chapter is that summary statistics and generic image-processing operations (smoothing, volume averaging, statistical parameter maps) can silently mix together signals from neighboring but functionally unrelated tissue unless the underlying geometry of cortex is respected.

56.1 Why visualization matters

Anscombe’s quartet is a classic illustration of a general danger: four data sets with identical summary statistics (mean, variance, correlation) can look completely different once plotted (Figure 56.1). The same danger applies to brain data — two very different patterns of activity or tissue structure can share the same summary numbers, so visualizing the data directly, not just its statistics, is an essential check.

Spatial resolution compounds this problem in the brain because of cortical folding. Two points that are close together in physical (Euclidean) space can be far apart along the folded cortical sheet, on opposite banks of a sulcus (Figure 56.2). Any analysis that treats nearby voxels as necessarily related is implicitly assuming a spatial smoothness that the folded anatomy does not actually have.

56.2 Topology and visualization

A single MR slice can cut across a sulcus twice, sampling both banks in the same plane even though they are far apart along the cortical sheet — for example, a coronal slice through the calcarine sulcus includes tissue from both banks of the fold (Figure 56.3). This is the concrete anatomical reason topology matters: the natural neighborhood structure of the data is defined by the cortical surface, not by the Euclidean geometry of the acquired volume.

56.3 Matching data methods to what you are studying

Thinking about the natural shape and organization of the problem you are studying is important. Mathematical theorems about smoothing and averaging are very useful, but every problem has its own special features, and being sensitive to them is essential — spatial averaging in the brain is a good example. A smoothing kernel applied in volume space averages together whatever voxels fall within its radius, regardless of whether those voxels belong to the same fold of cortex.

The trouble is visible as soon as the kernel straddles a sulcus. Opposite banks of a sulcus can be very close together in volume space yet functionally distinct — one bank gray matter, the other white matter, or two banks belonging to different functional maps — so opposite sides of sulci can’t be distinguished by a purely volumetric criterion (Figure 56.7). The figure shown in-plane is only a 2D cross-section; the averaging itself happens in the full 3D volume, which makes the problem worse, not better.

Smoothing therefore averages together signal from tissue that may not belong together at all (Figure 56.8) — the same sulcus-crossing problem, now shown as it would appear after a smoothing kernel has been applied at several locations along a fold.

56.4 Statistical parameter mapping

Statistical parameter maps (SPMs) render a statistical test (for example, a t- or F-statistic computed at every voxel) as a color overlay on an anatomical image. An older convention in the literature — one that can still be found in some papers — displayed these overlays on a small number of 2D slices, which is a visualization choice worth being alert to when reading older figures, since it can make results look more (or less) spatially extensive than they are.

Spatial smoothing is applied before statistical testing for several distinct reasons, and it is worth separating them out:

  • (i) Matched filter theorem
  • (ii) Central limit theorem
  • (iii) Gaussian random field theory
  • (iv) Inter-subject averaging

By the matched filter theorem, the optimal smoothing kernel size matches the expected spatial size of the effect being tested — smoothing at the wrong scale reduces sensitivity.

By the central limit theorem, smoothing renders the noise distribution more Gaussian, which helps satisfy the assumptions behind parametric statistical tests.

Inferences about regional effects that rely on Gaussian random field theory assume the underlying statistical field is smooth; explicit smoothing helps satisfy that assumption.

When averaging across subjects — whose anatomy never aligns perfectly — more aggressive smoothing (e.g., 8 mm in fMRI, 16 mm in PET) is often needed to project individual results onto a common space.

Each of these is a legitimate reason to smooth, but each also trades away some spatial specificity — precisely the specificity that the sulcus-crossing problem above shows can matter a great deal in folded cortex.

56.5 Surface-based visualization

Visualization exists to communicate results clearly, and the choice of surface matters as much as the statistics. The same activation can look very different rendered on a small number of thick axial slices versus rendered on a 3D cortical surface reconstruction (Figure 56.13).

Cortical surface projections are commonly shown from standard viewpoints, such as the lateral surface and the ventral occipital surface, so that results from different studies and subjects can be compared directly (Figure 56.14, Figure 56.15).

A concrete example of why this matters: interpreting results shown in a conventional multi-slice format is hard, even for an experienced reader (Figure 56.16). The same visual word form area data, replotted on a 3D cortical surface reconstruction, is much easier to interpret — the contrast showing an increasing response as a word form becomes more visible is immediately legible as a single, spatially coherent region rather than scattered patches across several slices (Figure 56.17).

56.6 Building the cortical surface

To build a representation of the cortical surface, the anatomical MRI volume is segmented into gray and white matter, and the boundary between them is identified. A variety of software tools do this segmentation; here the boundary is shown as identified by itkGray, one of several available pipelines (others include FreeSurfer) (Figure 56.18; Carman et al. (1995); Dale et al. (1999); Fischl et al. (1999); Wandell et al. (2000)).

Automatic segmentation is an excellent first approximation and very useful in practice (Figure 56.19), but there are always imperfections that need to be checked and, often, hand-corrected (Figure 56.20).

Once the voxels are classified as gray or white matter, they must be connected into a well-formed graph before a surface mesh can be built. Gray matter nodes are considered connected if they share a common parent voxel or if their parents are connected, and the classification, connectivity, and resulting cortical graph are all closely linked (Figure 56.21).

A well-formed white-matter classification must also avoid topological defects — cavities (holes enclosed by white matter) and handles (spurious connections that create a loop where none should exist). The Euler number is a standard way of detecting the presence of such defects (Figure 56.22; Toriwaki & Yonekura (2002)).

56.7 From classified voxels to a mesh

Graphics programs represent surfaces as meshes: a set of vertices (points) and edges (connections between them). A mesh can be built directly from the boundary of the classified white-matter voxels (Figure 56.23).

Representing the cortex as a mesh matters for more than visualization: the same mesh that renders the surface for viewing (in tools such as MeshLab) also encodes the gray-matter connectivity graph used for measurement, so a properly connected mesh is required both for pictures that look right and for distances and areas that are computed correctly (Figure 56.24).

A raw mesh built directly from voxel boundaries is jagged; 3D graphical tools (such as VTK, the Visualization Toolkit) can smooth the mesh without destroying the topological neighborhood relations of the surface (Figure 56.25). Modern segmentation and meshing tools such as itkGray increasingly build in this kind of anatomical and topological knowledge automatically, though getting good results still requires practice (Figure 56.26).

56.8 Curvature and extreme smoothing

Shading a rendered surface by local curvature makes the gyri and sulci easy to read at a glance: curvature is inversely related to the radius of the circle that best fits the local surface (Figure 56.27). Even after very heavy smoothing, the mesh’s topology — its neighborhood connectivity — remains unchanged; only the geometry (the shape) is altered, not the underlying graph structure (Figure 56.28).

56.9 Flat maps

Because the cortical sheet is topologically close to a 2D surface, it can be cut and unfolded into a flat map — a representation that preserves neighborhood relations along the cortical surface far better than any single volumetric slice can. Flattened representations are especially useful for displaying data that vary smoothly across the surface, such as eccentricity maps from retinotopic mapping experiments (Figure 56.29, Figure 56.30, Figure 56.31).

No flattening is perfect, however. The cortical surface has intrinsic curvature, which limits how well distances measured on the flattened image can match true distances on the folded surface — even a surface that is basically flat, and not strongly curved like a sphere, will not be perfectly representable on a plane without some distortion (Figure 56.32). The next chapter takes up the flattening procedure itself in detail.
